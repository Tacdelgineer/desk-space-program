Your GPU isn't slow.It's starving.

Inside a DGX Spark, the model lives in the memory chips. To write each token, the whole model crosses the memory bus to the GPU. The GPU does its math in a flash, then waits for the next delivery.

What's real on this page

  • The specs are published figures. DGX Spark: 128 GB of shared memory on a 256-bit bus, 273 GB/s, a 20-core Arm CPU next to a Blackwell GPU. RTX 5090: 32 GB of GDDR7 in 16 chips on a 512-bit bus, 1,792 GB/s. Mac Studio M3 Ultra: 256 GB on the chip package, a 1,024-bit bus, 819 GB/s. AMD Strix Halo (AMD's own mini PC with the Ryzen AI Max+ 395): 128 GB of LPDDR5X-8000 on a 256-bit bus, 256 GB/s, 16 Zen 5 cores on two chiplets and a Radeon 8060S GPU with 40 compute units on the third die, 120 W. RTX Pro 6000 Blackwell Workstation Edition: the 5090's GB202 chip with 188 of its 192 SMs switched on (the 5090 has 170), 96 GB of GDDR7 in 32 chips, 16 on each side of the board, on the same 512-bit bus, 1,792 GB/s, 600 W.
  • On Linux the Strix Halo's GPU may use only about half of the memory until a kernel setting raises the limit; the lab assumes the usual setting for local AI, 120 GB. Its compute figure is our estimate from AMD's numbers (40 compute units at 2.9 GHz): about 59 TFLOPS of 16-bit math, drawn as a GPU about half the size of the Spark's.
  • The outer shells are stylized, not the real designs: no logos, no exact shapes. The coffee mug beside each machine is the same mug, 82 mm across and 95 mm tall, drawn at that machine's real scale, so it looms over the 150 mm boxes and shrinks next to the 30 cm graphics cards.
  • The boards are illustrations, not teardowns. Placement, part counts and the coolers are simplified. Each glowing cell on a memory chip is 1 GB, and each glowing lane of the bus is 32 bits wide, so you can count them: 8, 16, 32, 8 and 16. The RTX Pro 6000's lanes each feed two chips, one on top of the board and one underneath, so its 16 memory spots hold 6 cells.
  • A graphics card sits in a PC, and llama.cpp keeps a model's lookup table in the PC's own memory, so on the 5090 and the Pro 6000 only the rest has to fit on the card. That is how Flash-Next's 111 GB file runs on a 96 GB card. The Pro 6000's 16-bit math is NVIDIA's figure with 32-bit adding, 504 TFLOPS; GeForce cards like the 5090 do that at half rate.
  • The crew dial runs several requests at once. Each step reads the weights once for everyone plus each request's prompt memory, and does the math once per request, so the total speed climbs until the GPU's math catches up with memory or the prompt memory no longer fits.
  • The five models' sizes come from their own checkpoints: parameters added up from the tensor shapes, and the prompt memory from each config (how many layers keep a cache, how many key-value heads, how wide). Qwen3.6 and Qwen3.8 keep a growing cache on only a quarter of their layers; the rest keep a small fixed state. Gemma 4 E4B shares its cache across 18 of its 42 layers.
  • Amber cells are a lookup table: Flash-Next's n-gram table (51B parameters) and Gemma's per-layer embeddings. They sit in memory, but each token reads only a few rows, so they barely flicker. On the Spark the table stays in CPU memory, mapped from the file.
  • Qwen3.8-Max (2.4 trillion parameters, 93B read per token) is an estimate only. Nothing here can hold it.
  • Model sizes between the presets are dense models whose prompt memory is scaled from an older 8B model.
  • Estimated speeds come from simple rules. Writing speed is bandwidth divided by the bytes read per token. Reading speed is compute divided by model size, and the compute figures are rough placeholders.
  • Measured speeds replace the estimates where they exist: the DGX Spark, 4-bit, the question and document prompts, one request.
  • Live mode (this page served by the bridge on the Spark): every number tagged measured is real. The wait before the first token is the reading, each token that arrives lights one pulse and types one piece of the answer, tokens per second is counted from the tokens as they arrive (the final number is llama-server's own), and the memory cells show the memory actually in use (1 cell = 1 GiB; the grey cells are other programs, the dim blue ones the part Linux never sees). GPU math used can't be measured, so live mode shows the GPU's power draw from nvidia-smi instead, and its temperature. The GPU blocks still light up by the estimate; the bus shows its published width and speed. This page draws on the GPU too: open on the Spark's own screen at 1080p, it slowed Gemma 4 E4B from 61 to 44 tokens per second (measured with and without it). The benchmark stops drawing the machine while it runs; open the lab from another computer for the fastest chat.
  • The system keeps about 8 GB for itself, shown as the dim cells.
  • GPU math used is the share of the GPU's math capacity in use. nvidia-smi shows a much higher busy figure while writing, because it counts any moment a program is running on the GPU, even one that mostly waits for memory.
  • Not modeled yet: heat, and the extra work of attention on very long prompts.

In the real series the Spark's numbers are measured on the machine. The Strix Halo's, and one of the RTX Pro 6000's, come from other people's published runs, tagged reported. Other numbers stay marked as estimated until they're measured.

Camera and keys

The console in front of the machine does everything: pads pick the machine, the long fader sets the model size, the jog wheel the crew, the lever the compression, the switches the prompt, the LID key opens and closes the machine. Lift the red cover and flip the switch to launch. Drag, or scroll over a control. The open machine's lid hangs above it: drag it back down to close it.
